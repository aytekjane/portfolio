LIGHTING: ÇEŞM-İ BÜLBÜL WALL LAMP - OMNIA SERIES - PAŞABAHÇE (IN-HOUSE WORK: AUTOBAN)

“Çeşm-i Bülbül,” translated as “the eye of the nightingale,” is a traditional Ottoman glassmaking technique developed in the late 18th century. It is distinguished by its intricate, spiraling, multicolored cane (filigree) patterns that resemble the eye of a nightingale. The technique was created by the Mevlevi dervish Mehmed Dede during the reign of Sultan Selim III, drawing inspiration from Venetian glassmaking traditions. This project was developed as an effort to carry this unique art form into the future. It was designed for Paşabahçe’s Omnia special collection and was showcased during the launch event as well as in retail stores.

R&D: BRONZE AGE BOAT, 3D SCANNER, MUSEUM (IN-HOUSE WORK: NEW YORK UNIVERSITY ABU DHABI - DESIGN ENGINEER)

The REF-funded Má I Bronze Age Boat Project explored Bronze Age maritime technologies through a comprehensive analysis of archaeological, textual, iconographic, and ethnographic data. The project combined conventional research methodologies with indigenous knowledge systems, integrating expert shipwrights from the western Indian Ocean who specialize in traditional boatbuilding techniques. Their practical expertise provided critical insights that extended beyond academic research, enabling a more authentic reconstruction process and allowing comparisons between historical evidence and living traditions. Building on this foundation, the Má II Project focused on the experimental reconstruction of a full-scale Bronze Age reed boat. This initiative is a collaboration between the Dhakira Center for Heritage Studies at NYU Abu Dhabi, Zayed University, and the Abu Dhabi Department of Culture and Tourism. The project aims to deepen understanding of maritime technology and its role in facilitating trade, cultural exchange, and political networks across the Gulf and the western Indian Ocean between 4000 and 2000 years ago. During the Bronze Age, vessels constructed from reeds and wood, sealed with bitumen, navigated trade routes connecting the Fertile Crescent with regions such as Dilmun (Bahrain), Magan (modern UAE and Oman), and Meluhha (Indus Valley). Although no complete shipwrecks from this period have been discovered, extensive archaeological evidence—including cuneiform texts, pottery, iconography, and material remains—has informed the reconstruction of these vessels. The central outcome of the Má II Project was the design and construction of an 18-meter-long ship using historically accurate materials such as reeds, timber, palm fiber cordage, and bitumen. The vessel was built in collaboration with traditional shipwrights from Kerala, whose knowledge of indigenous construction techniques played a vital role in the process. The reconstructed ship offers valuable insights into the structure, durability, cargo capacity, and sailing performance of Bronze Age vessels from the Umm an-Nar period (2600–2000 BCE). As part of the research and development component of the project, I designed a custom photogrammetric 3D scanning system to digitally document the shipbuilding process. This system captured thousands of high-resolution images from multiple angles, enabling precise 3D reconstruction of construction techniques and material assemblies. The aim was to create a detailed digital archive that preserves intangible craftsmanship knowledge while supporting interdisciplinary collaboration. The project also incorporated sustainable architectural strategies during the construction phase. The building environment utilized technical fabric with UV-filtering properties and a geothermal cooling system, achieving effective climate control without reliance on conventional air conditioning. Following the success of the initial reconstruction, a larger vessel was later developed as a continuation of the project. This ship is currently exhibited at the Zayed National Museum and has received significant international media attention, including coverage by National Geographic.
