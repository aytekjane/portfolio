INTERIOR: KILIMANJARO BAR ISTANBUL (IN-HOUSE WORK: AUTOBAN)

Located inside the Bomonti Historic Brewery, Kilimanjaro is a casual restaurant bar that is an integral part of Bomontiada. The project received the Time Out Istanbul “Best Restaurant” award. The structure was fabricated on site by shipyard craftsmen, reflecting the close relationship between developable surface logic and shipbuilding techniques. On site support during construction included guidance on structural alignment, correct placement, and precise assembly of all components.

EXTERIOR: ODAKULE PASSAGE CONCEPT

Odakule is a key urban passage with heavy pedestrian flow throughout the day, bringing together diverse user groups. The project combines the idea of movement and stepping inherent in the concept of a passage with the production-oriented vision of İstanbul Sanayi Odası, aiming to generate energy from human motion. A piezoelectric flooring system converts each step into kinetic energy. With high daily foot traffic, this creates a meaningful and continuous energy source. The generated energy powers a helical interactive lighting element, establishing a real-time connection between the user and the space. This project was developed in collaboration with Enrico Şansal for the Odakule Passage National Design Competition. As a result, the passage transforms from a simple transit area into an interactive, energy-producing urban experience.

R&D: BRONZE AGE BOAT, 3D SCANNER, MUSEUM (IN-HOUSE WORK: NEW YORK UNIVERSITY ABU DHABI - DESIGN ENGINEER)

The REF-funded Má I Bronze Age Boat Project explored Bronze Age maritime technologies through a comprehensive analysis of archaeological, textual, iconographic, and ethnographic data. The project combined conventional research methodologies with indigenous knowledge systems, integrating expert shipwrights from the western Indian Ocean who specialize in traditional boatbuilding techniques. Their practical expertise provided critical insights that extended beyond academic research, enabling a more authentic reconstruction process and allowing comparisons between historical evidence and living traditions. Building on this foundation, the Má II Project focused on the experimental reconstruction of a full-scale Bronze Age reed boat. This initiative is a collaboration between the Dhakira Center for Heritage Studies at NYU Abu Dhabi, Zayed University, and the Abu Dhabi Department of Culture and Tourism. The project aims to deepen understanding of maritime technology and its role in facilitating trade, cultural exchange, and political networks across the Gulf and the western Indian Ocean between 4000 and 2000 years ago. During the Bronze Age, vessels constructed from reeds and wood, sealed with bitumen, navigated trade routes connecting the Fertile Crescent with regions such as Dilmun (Bahrain), Magan (modern BAE and Umman) and Meluhha (Indus Valley). Although no complete shipwrecks from this period have been discovered, extensive archaeological evidence—including cuneiform texts, pottery, iconography, and material remains—has informed the reconstruction of these vessels. The central outcome of the Má II Project was the design and construction of an 18-meter-long ship using historically accurate materials such as reeds, timber, palm fiber cordage, and bitumen. The vessel was built in collaboration with traditional shipwrights from Kerala, whose knowledge of indigenous construction techniques played a vital role in the process. The reconstructed ship offers valuable insights into the structure, durability, cargo capacity, and sailing performance of Bronze Age vessels from the Umm an-Nar period (2600–2000 BCE). As part of the research and development component of the project, I designed a custom photogrammetric 3D scanning system to digitally document the shipbuilding process. This system captured thousands of high-resolution images from multiple angles, enabling precise 3D reconstruction of construction techniques and material assemblies. The aim was to create a detailed digital archive that preserves intangible craftsmanship knowledge while supporting interdisciplinary collaboration. The project also incorporated sustainable architectural strategies during the construction phase. The building environment utilized technical fabric with UV-filtering properties and a geothermal cooling system, achieving effective climate control without reliance on conventional air conditioning. Following the success of the initial reconstruction, a larger vessel was later developed as a continuation of the project. This ship is currently exhibited at the Zayed National Museum and has received significant international media attention, including coverage by National Geographic.

R&D: VENTILATION DEVICE

Reverse engineering and production of a ventilator originally designed by Japanese anesthesiologist Naoyuki Ishikita for use on the ISS, developed in response to potential ventilator shortages during the pandemic. During this period, major Turkish home appliance manufacturers and technology companies, such as Arçelik, Vestel, and Baykar, rapidly initiated efforts to mass-produce mechanical ventilators. As a contingency plan for triaged patients, a task force was established under ETMK (Industrial Designers’ Society of Turkey), bringing together multidisciplinary teams to develop alternative solutions. Following extensive testing and evaluation, the most functional and manufacturable design was selected for further development and potential production.

HOBBY: FRESHWATER HIGH-TECH PLANTED AQUARIUM DESIGN

These projects showcase a meticulously planned freshwater high-tech planted aquarium that merges advanced aquatic technology with natural aquascaping artistry. The design features a fully planted layout supported by high-intensity LED lighting, pressurized CO₂ injection, and automated nutrient dosing to promote vibrant, healthy plant growth. Careful attention was given to hardscape composition, plant selection, and flow dynamics, resulting in a balanced ecosystem that is both visually striking and biologically stable. Utilizing principles of biomimicry and Voronoi diagrams, I designed and 3D-printed modular structures of varying sizes that resemble mountainous formations. Their perforated surfaces allow water to flow naturally through the structures, mimicking organic currents, while internal cavities are filled with highly porous volcanic rock. These materials support aerobic bacteria on outer surfaces and anaerobic bacteria within inner pores, working together to biologically filter the water by converting toxic ammonia and nitrite into nitrate—an essential nutrient for aquatic plants. Surrounding these printed structures, mosses were carefully attached using transparent fishing line, allowing them to anchor and thrive by utilizing available nutrients. Throughout the process, I conducted experiments with various plant and moss species under different environmental conditions, including pH, KH, GH, conductivity, nutrient levels, CO₂ concentration, lighting duration, and Kelvin values. This integrated system promotes natural plant growth while enhancing biological filtration, resulting in a self-sustaining, bacteria-driven water purification ecosystem.
